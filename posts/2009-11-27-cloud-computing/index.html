Cloud computing

November 27, 2009 2-minute read
Technology

In the office, we have had quite a few heated discussions on the advent of cloud computing, and how it will change the computing landscape over the next decade. And it’s already happening as we speak.It all started when Google launched their Chromium OS, and one of us decided to compile the source code and stick it into a USB thumbdrive. We watched the OS boot up in approximately 12 seconds, and was presented with a beautiful simplistic interface - the web browser.One of my colleagues then asked what the point of this was, when you could very well run a thick OS instead of a thin one, and have the advantages of being able to run thick applications at the same time. With a thin OS, you could only run applications off the web interface, nothing more, nothing less.I believe this argument will not die off, but will continue to stay with us as long as computers are a part of our lives. Just like how men always argue that the women say that their husbands never listen to them. :)Although the push for cloud computing presents certain risks such as security and accessibility, it represents a conceptual leap in that applications and services are now not necessarily part of your organisational domain, and that these very apps and services could be offered by some entity outside of the domain.And it’s not just Google moving into this. Take, for example, an educational instituion. A school may say, “hey, let’s host some applications and deliver them through the web for the students to use at home.” In this case, the school becomes the application provider, and thus, an agent in the whol arena of cloud computing. And I already know of some schools delivering these services.Whether that approach is an appropriate one remains to be seen.
