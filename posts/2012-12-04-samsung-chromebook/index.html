Samsung Chromebook

December 4, 2012 4-minute read
Technology

The Samsung Chromebook has been one of the more interesting purchases I’ve made this year. I started off with the Google ecosystem in mid 2004, using the Google search functionality and graduating to its email soon after. Before I knew it, I was becoming more and more entrenched in using its various services.Buying the Chromebook was a natural extension to this. Before I bought it, I did a bit of research. It seemed that Google intended its use to be a secondary computer, something that you would take around the home and use when the primary computer was off. To me, this made sense - there was no use duplicating the function of the primary machine.Now having the Chromebook in my hand and using it for 5 days, I will safely say that it serves the purpose of beng that secondary computer very well. Its form factor allowed me to use it in bed instead of being chained to the desk. As there was no internal cooling fan, I could use it until the wee hours of the morning without waking up my wife. The long battery life gave me the ability to use it without recharging for extended periods of time.Now you already get all those advantages with either a netbook or a tablet. What then is the difference amongst the three?Let’s start with the tablet. One of my biggest gripes with the tablet form factor is that it is an entertainment device first and foremost. Content creation is an afterthought, and until people find a way to make content creation easier on tablets, we will continue to have difficulty typing on glass. The Chromebook, on the other hand, has a laptop keyboard that looks suspiciously like that of the MacBook Air. That makes serious work on the Chromebook a complete joy.Another issue is the platform. Android as an operating system caters to both mobile phones and tablets, and there are definitely differences in UI design between the two. I am finding a lack of quality tablet applications as opposed to phone ones - its seems to me that most applications are designed for devices with small screen estate such as phones. While you can run phone apps on a tablet, the inefficiencies of doing so become apparent immediately once the app is opened. There is so much screen real estate in a tablet - and phone apps don’t take advantage of that.I had chanced upon a netbook before and while I found the form factor acceptable, its reliance on the x86 chip architecture meant that it was besottled with all the issues of that platform. This meant a hot running chipset, noise coming from the cooling fan and the excess baggage of a 20 year old hardware and software architecture. Intel has tried to rectify this to varying degrees of success with the Ultrabook initative but I don’t think they are going to go that far with it. A new methodology of using the Web requires a new approach from both hardware and software perspectives.Which is why I was so excited about the ARM Chromebook. It was the embodiment of the same form factor I was used to but coupled with a new (and old) usage philosophy. I say new and old too because actually the ARM processors have been around for quite a while but have matured to a point where it is today. The Chromebook’s minimalistic software is just enough to run the hardware and the browser and that’s it. That shows in the system’s boot times - I clocked it at 12 seconds from black screen to logon screen. It’s that fast.The Chromebook has supplanted my tablet as the main means of computing away from the desktop, but even more importantly, it has changed my entire thinking around local computing resources versus computing-as-a-service.We have definitely come a long way since the days of having to upgrade your system constantly. And I’m glad we have.
